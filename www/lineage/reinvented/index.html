Indentation-stripping heredocs are the clearest case of Perl 6 choosing the industry's eventual answer first, and the one case where the choice demonstrably travelled. Apocalypse 2 accepted RFC 162's option of removing whitespace equal to the terminator's indentation in May 2001 (A249). Swift 4 took that rule from Perl 6 through the SE-0168 authors, as the borrowings section shows; PHP 7.3, Java 15 and C# 11 settled on the same closing-delimiter rule more than fifteen years after A2 without citing Perl 6, even though a 2016 Roslyn comment had put a q:to-style heredoc to the C# team (roslyn #223950). Java's text-block JEP cites requests for Swift's literals, so a Perl 6 → Swift → Java path is possible, but nothing documents it (JEP 3782). Ruby 2.3 used a different, least-indented-line rule taken from Rails (#90985). Perl 5.26's <<~ is the telling hybrid, and it is documented after all: its commit credits nobody, but Matthew Horsfall opened the June 2016 p5p thread by proposing to follow Perl 6's stripping rules and said he had modelled the spelling lightly on Ruby 2.3; p5p then made mismatched indentation fatal where Raku warns and continues, so the rule that shipped is stricter than either source (commit cdd637551; msg23747752; msg23751053; perldiag54). Ranges follow a different shape. Swift's internal release notes record its half-open operator moving from Ruby's ... in February 2014 through a swap in May to ..< on 23 June 2014, which they call precedented in Groovy; Groovy had adopted ..< in April 2005 from Shijun He's proposal of 10 January 2005, which mentions only Ruby, and Kotlin's YouTrack request named Swift as its model (Swift notes7; groovy-dev6; GroovyJSR notes55; KT-1561310). Raku's ..^ (A12, April 2004) is not in that chain. C#, Ruby, Dart and Zig are the languages where Raku's start- and end-exclusive range operators were put in front of the designers and not taken up, and in Rust Perl 6's ..^ served as an argument against using that spelling for the inclusive range (csharplang #18556; #1213357; dart-lang/language #210558; zig#35959; rust#2823760), and Groovy's <..<, the closest functional match to ^..^, appears in the Kotlin KEEP only as Groovy prior art (KEEP9).

Implicit parameters split into three families elsewhere, and none cites Raku. By semantics — this pairing is our analysis, not a claim any source makes — Raku's positional placeholders ($^a) correspond to the numbered-hole family of Swift's $0, Ruby's _1, Elixir's &1 and Clojure's %1; Groovy's, Kotlin's and Ruby 3.4's it, a single named parameter that may be used repeatedly ({ it * it }), corresponds to Raku's $_ in a bare block, whose ancestor is Perl 5's $_ in map and grep; and Scala's _, where each occurrence is a fresh parameter (_ * _ takes two), is the analogue of WhateverCode. The documented origins point elsewhere. James Strachan added it to Groovy on 29 October 2003 with no stated source, and Andrey Breslav's talk on Kotlin's ancestry credits it to Groovy (c525bfb13; Breslav16). Swift's $0 began as _0/_1 in Lattner's July 2010 commit and was renamed on 18 September 2010 because Bertrand, almost certainly Bertrand Serlet, preferred $0 (667078661; 1bef5bd15; Lattner 201962). Scala's _ + 1 arrived in version 2.5 in May 2007 (Scala changelog14), more than five years after $^a and eight months after Whatever entered the spec, but neither the changelog nor the commits up to the v2.5.0 tag give a rationale (scala v2.5.063); Ruby's tickets mention Perl only as a readability warning (#1898018; #1572364). Named-argument punning is the closest miss on the syntax side: Rust RFC 1682 considered a prefix-colon S { :x } form, nearly Raku's :$x, and attributed it to MoonScript (RFC 168219), while PEP 736's prior-art survey covers Ruby, ReasonML, SystemVerilog, Jakt, OCaml, JavaScript and Rust but not Raku (PEP 73621).

Signatures and CLIs show how timing alone misleads. PEP 3102 added keyword-only arguments in April 2006, three years after A6 and a year before PEP 3133 proved that Python 3000 designers were reading Perl 6; yet it names no other language (PEP 310223), and the python-3000 threads that produced it settle the question. Their only Perl 6 remark is Guido's dismissal of a bracketed parameter syntax as emulating Perl 6, a day before he pronounced the bare *; nobody mentioned A6's named-only parameters, and the later PEP 3102 threads name no other language at all, so the verdict is independent invention (00109465; 00115066; python-dev67). Rakudo shipped MAIN and USAGE in its 2010.06 release (Rakudo ChangeLog68); Michele Simionato's plac, which also generates a command line from a function signature, reached PyPI on 2 June 2010 — the same month (plac25), and every later tool in that line, through argh, clize, Fire, Typer and Nim's cligen, descends from the Python side (cligen26). On paper Perl 6 was four years ahead: Larry Wall specified MAIN, multi-dispatch over MAIN candidates, signature-generated usage text and a USAGE override in one S06 commit on 10 August 2006, crediting only Unix switch conventions and Perl 5's unless caller idiom, and Rakudo ran a basic MAIN from 11 November 2008 (msg2579569; 41d13f870). Both requests to copy it that turned up were refused: Ruby's developer meeting rejected a Perl 6-like main in July 2012, and Go's Brad Fitzpatrick declined one in 2016 (#501171; go#1717872).

Gradual typing is where Perl 6 came closest to acknowledgement and still received none. Jeremy Siek's explainer names Perl 6 alongside Python 3k and ES4 as languages gradual typing should give a foundation to (Siek73), but the 2006 Siek & Taha paper itself mentions Common Lisp, Dylan, Cecil, Bigloo, Strongtalk and Thatte, not Perl (Siek & Taha40). Perl 6's model — annotations add checked constraints and an unannotated variable means Any — is closer to Common Lisp-style optional typing than to Siek's sound-at-the-boundary gradual typing, so Wikipedia's uncited claim that Raku had gradual typing from the start is true only in the loose sense (Wikipedia: Gradual typing74). The P0515 survey for C++20's <=> is a small but telling data point: it asserts that among major languages only Haskell returns a type from three-way comparison, overlooking Raku's Order enum (P0515R048), which fits the broader finding that designers' surveys rarely look at Raku at all.
