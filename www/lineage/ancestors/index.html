Roles

Roles are the best-documented case of a shared ancestor: Perl 6 and the others both took them from the Traits paper.

  • A12 accepts the Traits paper's argument that classes should not manage both objects and code reuse, and lists that paper as its only reference (A121).
  • Perl 6 extended the idea in three ways. Roles may hold state, which Larry Wall defended on perl6-language in December 2003. Conflicts are not resolved by renaming or excluding methods. And roles can be applied at runtime, as mixins (perl6-language35).
  • The Traits authors then listed Perl 6 roles as an adopter in their 2006 TOPLAS paper, next to Scala (TOPLAS 200636).
  • PHP, Java 8 and Groovy cite the Traits papers or the mixin tradition. Java's designers described default methods as a limited, stateless form of traits (FOOL 20113).
What the sources say 2

  1. Larry Wall, Perl 6 language designer, replying to Stéphane Payrard perl6-language, Vocabulary thread 12–13 Dec 2003 source ↗

    Answering a remark that the Smalltalk traits paper made roles stateless, notes its authors meant to study state and says a role may also supply attributes.

  2. Stéphane Ducasse, Oscar Nierstrasz, Nathanael Schärli, Roel Wuyts and Andrew Black, the Traits authors ACM TOPLAS 28(2), Traits: A Mechanism for Fine-Grained Reuse Mar 2006 source ↗

    Lists traits as standard in Scala and implemented for Perl 5, and reports work under way to build them into Perl 6, where they are called roles.

Multiple dispatch

Here the shared ancestor is CLOS:

  • Julia's 2012 paper places itself with CLOS, Dylan, Cecil and Diesel (Julia 20125).
  • Stefan Karpinski later said that even CLOS was not a motivation, and that Julia's resemblance to Fortress came from shared problems (julia-dev37).
What the sources say 1

  1. Stefan Karpinski, Julia co-creator julia-dev mailing list 29 Jun 2013 source ↗

    Says resembling CLOS was never a goal for Julia and that its likeness to Fortress arose because both languages faced similar problems rather than from mutual inspiration.

Element-wise operators

Perl 6's hyper-operators and Julia's dots both mark element-wise application in the syntax, rather than vectorising implicitly. The two languages reached the same principle:

  • A3 made that choice in 2001, with a joke about APL (A338).
  • Julia made it in 2017, contrasting itself with Chapel and citing Matlab and NumPy (Julia blog9).

  1. Larry Wall, Perl 6 language designer Apocalypse 3, perl.com 2 Oct 2001 source ↗

    Accepts RFC 82's element-wise operators as hyper operators marked by a leading caret, joking that APL is next, and has them stretch a scalar across a missing dimension.

For an implementer, Perl 5's PPC0004 draws the useful line. Raku phasers are compile-time declarations, active by their presence; defer is a statement that takes effect only if execution reaches it (PPC000442). The ENTER, FIRST, NEXT and LAST phasers have no counterpart in any mainstream language.

What the sources say 1

  1. Paul Evans, Perl core developer Perl PPC 0004, Deferred block syntax 16 Jun 2021 source ↗

    Explains the block was first named LEAVE, dropped because Raku's LEAVE behaves differently, and that Raku's phaser blocks take effect at compile time merely by being declared, whereas defer acts only once execution reaches it.

Graphemes

Raku specified grapheme-level characters in 2002, and shipped them in April 2015. Others shipped grapheme-aware strings in between, building on Unicode's UAX #29:

  • Elixir made String.length grapheme-aware in November 2012 (Elixir changelog21).
  • Swift built Character on grapheme clusters in 2014 (Swift book43).
  • Michael Ilseman later named Perl 6 as a peer in supporting grapheme-cluster regex semantics (forum #10044).
What the sources say 2

  1. Apple, The Swift Programming Language Strings and Characters chapter undated (current edition) source ↗

    States that each Swift Character value represents one extended grapheme cluster.

  2. Michael Ilseman, Apple's String and regex lead Swift Forums, Declarative String Processing Overview, post 100 6 Oct 2021 source ↗

    Notes that certain regex engines can match by canonical equivalence or by grapheme clusters, adding that he believes Perl 6 did.

NFG's distinctive contribution is still Raku's own: synthetic codepoints that give O(1) grapheme indexing (MoarVM strings45).

What the sources say 1

  1. Samantha McVey, MoarVM developer Strings in MoarVM, MoarVM docs Aug 2017 source ↗

    Describes MoarVM's NFG string form, which extends Unicode NFC, held in fixed-width arrays where negative values stand for synthetic graphemes of several codepoints.

  • Julia weighed NFG in writing during its 2016 string redesign. It judged NFG sensible only for strings that start as UTF-32, and costly in memory (#1438346).
  • In the same thread, a Perl 6 developer pointed out that the Parrot write-up under discussion had always been aspirational. So Julia's judgment rested partly on that older text, not on MoarVM (comment47).

Definedness smileys

The :D/:U smileys arrived after Spec# and Eiffel's attached types. They are a binder-checked definedness constraint, not a sum type or a flow analysis, so they are a different design from null safety rather than an early form of it (04840a348).

What the sources say 1

  1. Larry Wall, Perl 6 language designer Raku/old-design-docs commit 04840a3 (S12) 26 Jun 2009 source ↗

    Adds the Int:D type modifier to S12, restricting a parameter or variable to defined Int instances.

Grammars and PEGs

Here the vocabulary came into Perl 6 later. Ford's 2004 PEG paper does not mention Perl (Ford32), and Larry Wall described Perl 6 patterns as a native PEG implementation in S05 on 5 May 2010 (52d207433).
